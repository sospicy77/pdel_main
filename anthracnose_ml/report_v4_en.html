The index Vulnerability = effective exposure × sensitivity × (1 − adaptive capacity) discriminates occurrence with AUC 0.84.
  • Severity depends most on immediate removal of infected fruit (severity ×0.19) and anthracnose last year (×1.9); farms with more protectant sprays had less damage. First-symptom DOY and days transplant→first symptom were removed from the predictors because they describe the same thing as the response (disease progress). Among 15 regression models, ExtraTrees (15 variables, R² 0.39) did best; the nested CV R², free of selection bias, is 0.33 (Sections 07–09).

01Data and design

  • Changes from v3 (v4): Y25M005 (disease-free) removed; Y25M013 severity 0.05 → 0.25; Y25M018 immediate removal 1 → 0; Y25M033 Jul–Aug max days since spray before rain 4 → 8. 141 farms in total.
  • Occurrence label: Instead of the Y1 occurrence label, we defined occurrence (1) as Y2 total severity > threshold and everything else as 0. A threshold of 0 equals the original Y1.
  • Classification predictors: All variables from cultivar resistance to exposure, excluding first-symptom DOY, days transplant→first symptom and immediate removal (recorded only for diseased farms). The all-variable model has 44 candidates without exposure (including 8 weather variables and 2 IR-area variables); the no-weather model has 35 (exposure is the only weather information).
  • Procedure: Screening per threshold (2 variable sets × 2 selection methods × 4 models × 6 values of k, 5-fold × 5 repeats) → at the best threshold, 4 selection methods (mean rank, RF-SHAP, RFE, Boruta) × 6 models × k (5-fold × 6 repeats, selection inside each fold) → the final model is the setting with the fewest variables within 0.005 of the best AUC.

Changing the threshold changes the prevalence (43% → 28%) and what is being predicted, so AUC differences mean "which definition the predictors separate better". 03Occurrence model: all variables (without exposure)

Exposure is used separately as the exposure (E) term of the vulnerability analysis in Section 06, so this model leaves exposure out and uses the remaining 44 variables (including 8 weather variables and 2 IR-area variables). Occurrence = severity > 0.03 (40 farms). Final model: RF-SHAP selected 20 variables · Random Forest. AUC with variable selection inside each fold 0.800; AUC with the selected variables fixed 0.816. The same procedure with exposure included (45 variables) gave AUC 0.806, so dropping exposure barely reduced predictive power (−0.006). The information carried by exposure is taken over by the weather variables (rainfall, temperature, rainy days) and the rain–spray timing variables.

The two measures answer different questions. Stability-selection frequency asks "is the variable picked consistently when only a few are chosen among many correlated candidates?"; the Firth odds ratio asks "how large is the effect after adjusting for the pre-specified variables?". Within each outer training fold, the best of the 45 pairs was chosen by inner CV and then evaluated on the outer fold (outer 5-fold × 4). AUC was 0.779 without a pair and 0.781 with the chosen pair, almost no difference, and the chosen pair varied: 14 different pairs across 20 folds. There is no firm evidence that any particular pair is best, so rather than picking a pair from the screen we recommend the pre-specified June mean temperature + July–August rainfall. July–August humidity and rainy days should be confirmed with data from another year before use.

06Vulnerability analysis: exposure × sensitivity × (1 − adaptive capacity)

We fitted a model of the occurrence probability (severity > 3%) with the same structure as the vulnerability formula. From it we computed the reduction in occurrence probability per practice, each variable's contribution, how practice effects change with exposure, and an adaptive-capacity index.

  • Exposure (E): the exposure value. Sensitivity (S): disease last year, continuous cropping and a pepper field within 50 m (conditions a farm cannot change in the short term), plus farm size (no. of plants) as an adjustment. Adaptive capacity (A): two practices, days since spray before rain and weed mat (see the comparison table below for the selection).
  • Practice score a (0 = worst, 1 = best): weed mat used = 1. Days since spray before rain (Jul–Aug max): 27 or more → 0, 2 or fewer → 1, linear in between.
  • Model: P(occurrence) = 1 − exp(−H), H = λ · Eα · S(E) · (1 − A). Adaptive capacity starts from the rule that practices act independently, A = 1 − Π(1 − wj·aj); a synergy or overlap term between the two practices was tested with a likelihood-ratio test. Uncertainty comes from 300 bootstrap resamples.

  • Spray intensity acts through timing. Whenever days since spray before rain was in the model, the weights of the spray-intensity variables (spray days, July spray count, index) were 0 in every set. Without it, spray intensity took over part of the effect (weight 0.34–0.37), but performance fell to 0.70 (S7, S8). More sprays shorten the days before rain (ρ −0.57 to −0.63), so what matters is whether fungicide was on the plants just before rain, more than the number of sprays.
  • Taking the SHAP ranking at face value (S2) did worst (AIC 155.8). The six spray variables repeat the same information (correlations 0.6–0.9), and RF splits importance among them, so several reach the top individually. RF SHAP also tends to rate binary variables (weed mat) low. SHAP is useful for finding which group of variables matters (the spray-intensity group has the largest combined SHAP, 0.122), but it is not the right criterion for choosing the independent components of a vulnerability formula.

The spray-intensity index is the first principal component of the 6 standardized spray variables and explains 66% of their variance. Loadings: July spray count 0.43, Spray days 0.50, Protectant (M) sprays 0.39, June spray count 0.42, May spray count 0.30, Aug spray count 0.38.

Tests of the model structure

  • Exposure raises the risk only when inoculum is present. The exposure exponent α is 0.06, close to 0, and almost all of the exposure effect enters through the "disease last year × exposure" term (θx = 1.49, 95% interval 0.04–2.77). High-exposure farms with disease last year have an observed rate of 74% against a predicted 66%. Farms without disease last year have a current occurrence probability of 22–24% whether exposure is 0.1 or 0.9.
  • Multiplying by exposure linearly (α = 1) does not fit the data (CV AUC 0.64 vs 0.76). For the vulnerability calculation we therefore recommend the model's effective exposure, Eeff = Eα · exp(θx · disease last year · E), rescaled to 0–1, instead of the raw exposure value.
  • No synergy or overlap between the two practices was significant (p = 0.95). The independent-action rule 1 − Π(1 − w·a) is enough.

How much do practices lower the occurrence probability at high exposure?

Mean occurrence probability when every one of the 141 farms is set to exposure 0.1, 0.5 or 0.9, each farm keeps its own sensitivity, and only the practices change (g-computation). Parentheses show the reduction from current practice (pp) and the relative reduction; brackets show the bootstrap 95% interval of the reduction.

What "current practice" means. The practices the 141 farms actually reported in the survey, left as they are. Only exposure is set to the same value (0.1, 0.5 or 0.9) for every farm; practices (days since spray before rain, weed mat) and sensitivity (disease last year, continuous cropping, nearby pepper field, farm size) keep each farm's observed values. The predicted occurrence probabilities are then averaged over farms. It is therefore an average over the sampled farms, not the value for a particular farm or an assumed "standard practice".

Practices in the sample: weed mat used on 53% of farms; Jul–Aug max days since spray before rain median 8 days (interquartile range 5–14; only 7% at the best level of 2 days or fewer); mean adaptive-capacity score 0.68; 43% of farms had anthracnose last year. The other rows change only the named practice to its best level, and the difference from "current practice" is that practice's effect.

Why it is 23% even at exposure 0.1: exposure raises the risk only on farms with disease last year (α ≈ 0.06), so lowering exposure leaves the baseline risk from sensitivity and current practices. Vulnerability formula (occurrence = severity above 3%)

A = 1 − (1 − 0.84·adays since spray before rain)(1 − 0.50·aWeed mat)

Eeff = E0.06 · exp(1.49 · disease last year · E) / max

S = exp(0.15·Continuous cropping + 0.41·nearby pepper field − 0.46·sizez) / max

Vulnerability V = Eeff × S × (1 − A), occurrence probability ≈ 1 − exp(−λ·V′) (V′ = V before rescaling)

sizez = (log no. of plants − mean) / SD. A farm 1 SD larger has S multiplied by exp(−0.46) ≈ 0.63, an adjustment toward lower risk. Disease last year has a zero main-effect coefficient and enters only through its product with exposure (effective exposure).

Mean adaptive capacity is 0.68, lower on diseased farms (0.55) than on disease-free farms (0.73). The originally proposed form, exposure × (1 − adaptive capacity), discriminates with only AUC 0.63, while the form with effective exposure and sensitivity reaches 0.84 (training data), so we recommend the latter as the vulnerability index.

Limitations: (1) With only 40 events, the weight intervals are wide. The interval for days since spray before rain (0.04–1.0) excludes 0, while the lower bound for the weed mat sits at the boundary (0–0.05 depending on the bootstrap). (2) This is observational survey data, so the reductions are associations predicted by the model, not causal effects. (3) Extreme scenarios such as "all worst" extrapolate beyond the data and are left out of the tables.

10Conclusions

  • Occurrence threshold: The predictors separate farms best when occurrence is defined as severity above 3% (AUC 0.81). Mild disease (≤ 3%) is hard to tell apart from no disease with these predictors.
  • Occurrence risk factors: June temperature, July–August rainfall and disease last year raise the risk; a weed mat and spraying promptly around rain lower it. Using exposure alone instead of the weather variables gives nearly the same accuracy (AUC 0.79) as the weather variables without exposure (0.80), so an exposure-based model is the practical choice in the field.
  • Weather information: Exposure does not replace the weather variables. Adding June mean temperature and July–August rainfall to exposure in the occurrence model gives the same performance as all 10 weather variables (AUC 0.81).
  • Vulnerability: The highest-risk group is high-exposure farms that had anthracnose last year. On these farms, adaptive practices such as spraying shortly before rain (≤ 2 days) and using a weed mat lower the occurrence probability the most. Whether fungicide was on the plants just before rain matters more than the number of sprays. The vulnerability index should use effective exposure, which accounts for disease last year, rather than raw exposure.
  • Severity: Leaving out onset timing, immediate removal of infected fruit after onset reduces damage the most (×0.19), and farms with anthracnose last year have more damage (×1.9). The remaining cropping and environmental variables explain only about 33–39% of the variation in log severity, so much of the severity depends on onset timing and other factors not in the survey.
