01Data and design

  • Changes from v3 (v4): Y25M005 (disease-free) removed; Y25M013 severity 0.05 → 0.25; Y25M018 immediate removal 1 → 0; Y25M033 Jul–Aug max days since spray before rain 4 → 8. 141 farms in total.
  • Occurrence label: Instead of the Y1 occurrence label, we defined occurrence (1) as Y2 total severity > threshold and everything else as 0. A threshold of 0 equals the original Y1.
  • Classification predictors: All variables from cultivar resistance to exposure, excluding first-symptom DOY, days transplant→first symptom and immediate removal (recorded only for diseased farms). The all-variable model has 44 candidates without exposure (including 8 weather variables and 2 IR-area variables); the no-weather model has 35 (exposure is the only weather information).
  • Procedure: Screening per threshold (2 variable sets × 2 selection methods × 4 models × 6 values of k, 5-fold × 5 repeats) → at the best threshold, 4 selection methods (mean rank, RF-SHAP, RFE, Boruta) × 6 models × k (5-fold × 6 repeats, selection inside each fold) → the final model is the setting with the fewest variables within 0.005 of the best AUC.

02Choosing the occurrence threshold

{{t_thr}}

For both variable sets, AUC was highest at threshold 0.03 (severity above 3%), with 0.015 close behind (PR-AUC and balanced accuracy were highest at 0.015). Following the requested criterion (AUC), we chose 0.03.

Changing the threshold changes the prevalence (43% → 28%) and what is being predicted, so AUC differences mean "which definition the predictors separate better". Because 0.03 is the edge of the search range, a larger threshold could do better still. PR-AUC has a baseline equal to the prevalence, so AUC is the fairer comparison across thresholds.

Effect sizes (pre-specified model, {{ca_nspec}} variables)

Odds ratios estimated after selecting variables on the same data are inflated, and their p-values and confidence intervals are not valid. We therefore fixed the variables in advance, independent of the results, using the vulnerability framework (exposure, sensitivity, adaptive capacity; Section 05), but left exposure out of this model: three sensitivity variables (disease last year, continuous cropping, nearby pepper field), two adaptive practices (days since spray before rain and weed mat, selected as in Section 05), farm size as an adjustment, and two weather representatives (July–August rainfall, June mean temperature). With only {{ca_epv}} events per variable, we used Firth-penalized logistic regression, which reduces coefficient inflation.

{{ca_or}}

July–August rainfall (×1.90 per SD, p = 0.004), June mean temperature (×1.99, p = 0.013) and days since spray before rain (×1.91, p = 0.012) raised the risk, and weed mat (×0.32, p = 0.024) lowered it. The confidence intervals of the sensitivity variables and farm size include 1. The odds ratios are almost the same as in the 9-variable model with exposure, because there exposure had an odds ratio of 1.03 (p = 0.91) and added nothing once the weather variables were in. All VIFs are below 2.

The regularization range is limited so that on average at most 5 variables are selected; with a frequency threshold of 0.7 the expected number of falsely selected variables is at most {{ca_ev}}. Frequency ≥ 0.7 is "strong", 0.4–0.7 "moderate", below 0.4 "weak".

{{ca_stab}}

The two measures answer different questions. Stability-selection frequency asks "is the variable picked consistently when only a few are chosen among many correlated candidates?"; the Firth odds ratio asks "how large is the effect after adjusting for the pre-specified variables?". For example, the weed mat has a low frequency (0.30) but a significant adjusted effect (p = 0.02). Days since spray before rain (frequency 0.68, p = 0.012) and July–August rainfall (0.63, p = 0.004) are high on both and have the strongest evidence.

In observed rates, 11% of farms with ≤120 mm of July–August rainfall exceeded 3% damage, against 40% above 200 mm. Farms with disease last year: 40% (20% without); weed-mat users: 21% (36% without).

05Vulnerability analysis: exposure × sensitivity × (1 − adaptive capacity)

We fitted a model of the occurrence probability (severity > 3%) with the same structure as the vulnerability formula. From it we computed the reduction in occurrence probability per practice, each variable's contribution, how practice effects change with exposure, and an adaptive-capacity index.

  • Exposure (E): the exposure value. Sensitivity (S): disease last year, continuous cropping and a pepper field within 50 m (conditions a farm cannot change in the short term), plus farm size (no. of plants) as an adjustment. Adaptive capacity (A): two practices, days since spray before rain and weed mat (see the comparison table below for the selection).
  • Practice score a (0 = worst, 1 = best): weed mat used = 1. Days since spray before rain (Jul–Aug max): 27 or more → 0, 2 or fewer → 1, linear in between.
  • Model: P(occurrence) = 1 − exp(−H), H = λ · Eα · S(E) · (1 − A). Adaptive capacity starts from the rule that practices act independently, A = 1 − Π(1 − wj·aj); a synergy or overlap term between the two practices was tested with a likelihood-ratio test. Uncertainty comes from 300 bootstrap resamples.

Selecting the adaptive-capacity components: comparison of candidate sets

In the no-weather RF model, spray-intensity variables such as July spray count, spray days and protectant sprays had high SHAP importance, while the weed mat and days since spray before rain ranked lower. We therefore compared 9 candidate sets in the same vulnerability model, including the top SHAP variables, spray-intensity representatives (spray days, or an index of 6 spray variables) and the previous stability-selection set (repeated stratified 5-fold × 10; weights from 200 bootstrap resamples).

{{v_t_adapt}}
  • S9 (days since spray before rain + weed mat) had the best CV AUC (0.759) and AIC (148.3), and in this comparison it was the only set whose two weights both had 95% intervals excluding 0.
  • Spray intensity acts through timing. Whenever days since spray before rain was in the model, the weights of the spray-intensity variables (spray days, July spray count, index) were 0 in every set. Without it, spray intensity took over part of the effect (weight 0.34–0.37), but performance fell to 0.70 (S7, S8). More sprays shorten the days before rain (ρ −0.57 to −0.63), so what matters is whether fungicide was on the plants just before rain, more than the number of sprays.
  • Taking the SHAP ranking at face value (S2) did worst (AIC 155.8). The six spray variables repeat the same information (correlations 0.6–0.9), and RF splits importance among them, so several reach the top individually. RF SHAP also tends to rate binary variables (weed mat) low. SHAP is useful for finding which group of variables matters (the spray-intensity group has the largest combined SHAP, 0.122), but it is not the right criterion for choosing the independent components of a vulnerability formula.

The spray-intensity index is the first principal component of the 6 standardized spray variables and explains {{v_int_evr}}% of their variance. Loadings: {{v_int_load}}.

Tests of the model structure

  • Exposure raises the risk only when inoculum is present. The exposure exponent α is {{v_alpha}}, close to 0, and almost all of the exposure effect enters through the "disease last year × exposure" term (θx = {{v_thx}}, 95% interval 0.04–2.77). High-exposure farms with disease last year have an observed rate of 74% against a predicted 66%. Farms without disease last year have a current occurrence probability of 22–24% whether exposure is 0.1 or 0.9.
  • Multiplying by exposure linearly (α = 1) does not fit the data (CV AUC 0.64 vs 0.76). For the vulnerability calculation we therefore recommend the model's effective exposure, Eeff = Eα · exp(θx · disease last year · E), rescaled to 0–1, instead of the raw exposure value.
  • No synergy or overlap between the two practices was significant (p = 0.95). The independent-action rule 1 − Π(1 − w·a) is enough.
{{v_t_cv}}

The multiplicative model (0.759) matched or slightly beat the logistic model with exposure × practice interactions (0.743), and we chose it because it matches the vulnerability formula. It is below the full ML model (Section 04, 0.79), the price of keeping 7 variables and a fixed structure.

Parentheses show the reduction from current practice (pp) and the relative reduction; brackets show the bootstrap 95% interval of the reduction.

What "current practice" means. The practices the 141 farms actually reported in the survey, left as they are. Only exposure is set to the same value (0.1, 0.5 or 0.9) for every farm; practices (days since spray before rain, weed mat) and sensitivity (disease last year, continuous cropping, nearby pepper field, farm size) keep each farm's observed values. The predicted occurrence probabilities are then averaged over farms. It is therefore an average over the sampled farms, not the value for a particular farm or an assumed "standard practice".

Practices in the sample: weed mat used on 53% of farms; Jul–Aug max days since spray before rain median 8 days (interquartile range 5–14; only 7% at the best level of 2 days or fewer); mean adaptive-capacity score 0.68; 43% of farms had anthracnose last year. The other rows change only the named practice to its best level, and the difference from "current practice" is that practice's effect.

{{v_A_formula}}

Eeff = E{{v_alpha}} · exp({{v_thx}} · disease last year · E) / max

S = exp({{v_S_formula}}) / max

Vulnerability V = Eeff × S × (1 − A), occurrence probability ≈ 1 − exp(−λ·V′) (V′ = V before rescaling)

sizez = (log no. of plants − mean) / SD. A farm 1 SD larger has S multiplied by exp(−0.46) ≈ 0.63, an adjustment toward lower risk. Disease last year has a zero main-effect coefficient and enters only through its product with exposure (effective exposure).

{{v_t_auc}}

Mean adaptive capacity is {{v_A_mean}}, lower on diseased farms (0.55) than on disease-free farms (0.73). The originally proposed form, exposure × (1 − adaptive capacity), discriminates with only AUC 0.63, while the form with effective exposure and sensitivity reaches 0.84 (training data), so we recommend the latter as the vulnerability index.

Limitations: (1) With only 40 events, the weight intervals are wide. The interval for days since spray before rain (0.04–1.0) excludes 0, while the lower bound for the weed mat sits at the boundary (0–0.05 depending on the bootstrap). (2) This is observational survey data, so the reductions are associations predicted by the model, not causal effects. (3) Extreme scenarios such as "all worst" extrapolate beyond the data and are left out of the tables.

06Severity regression: model comparison (Y2)

We analysed the 60 diseased farms (severity > 0) of the v4 data. The response is Y2 total severity. First-symptom DOY and days transplant→first symptom were removed from the predictors. Earlier first symptoms naturally mean a longer epidemic and higher final severity, so these two variables describe the same thing as the response (disease progress) and cannot explain which cropping or environmental factors raise damage. Immediate removal of infected fruit is a management response after onset and stays as a predictor (36 candidates).

{{t_models}}

Removing the onset-timing variables lowered performance markedly (best R² 0.56 → 0.39), showing how much of the severity variation they carried. This time ExtraTrees (tuned, 15 variables, 0.392) did best, followed by default ExtraTrees (0.365), Random Forest (0.322) and CatBoost (0.308). Linear and kernel models (0.26–0.30) and the other boosting models (0.21–0.26) were lower. Without the strong linear signal of onset timing, tree models that capture non-linear and interaction effects of the remaining variables gained the edge. Averaging the top 3 models (0.375) did not beat the single best model.

R² (Y2 raw scale) converts log10 predictions back to proportions. RMSE is in severity proportion units.

Response scale and variable set

{{t_scale}}

Models trained on log10(Y2) were clearly better on the log-scale R². On the raw-scale R² alone, a model trained on raw Y2 with weather variables was slightly higher (0.34 vs 0.27), but its log-scale fit was much worse (0.16). Adding weather (set B) did not help on the log scale, so we used set A.

Interpretive linear model: how many times does severity change?

{{t_coef}}

OLS regression of log10 severity on the three core variables chosen by Kernel Ridge. Severity multiplier = 10^coefficient (other variables fixed). Farms that remove infected fruit immediately have about 0.19× the severity (95% interval 0.10–0.37), farms with anthracnose last year about 1.9× (1.07–3.27), and 1 SD (3.6) more protectant sprays gives 0.83×, which is not significant. In the raw data, the median severity on farms that remove infected fruit immediately is 2.0%, about one-eighth of that on farms that do not (16.8%).

09Conclusions

  • Occurrence threshold: The predictors separate farms best when occurrence is defined as severity above 3% (AUC 0.81). Mild disease (≤ 3%) is hard to tell apart from no disease with these predictors.
  • Occurrence risk factors: June temperature, July–August rainfall and disease last year raise the risk; a weed mat and spraying promptly around rain lower it. Using exposure alone instead of the weather variables gives nearly the same accuracy (AUC 0.79) as the weather variables without exposure (0.80), so an exposure-based model is the practical choice in the field.
  • Vulnerability: The highest-risk group is high-exposure farms that had anthracnose last year. On these farms, adaptive practices such as spraying shortly before rain (≤ 2 days) and using a weed mat lower the occurrence probability the most. Whether fungicide was on the plants just before rain matters more than the number of sprays. The vulnerability index should use effective exposure, which accounts for disease last year, rather than raw exposure.
  • Severity: Leaving out onset timing, immediate removal of infected fruit after onset reduces damage the most (×0.19), and farms with anthracnose last year have more damage (×1.9). The remaining cropping and environmental variables explain only about 33–39% of the variation in log severity, so much of the severity depends on onset timing and other factors not in the survey.
