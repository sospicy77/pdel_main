01Data and design

  • Changes from v3 (v4): Y25M005 (disease-free) removed; Y25M013 severity 0.05 → 0.25; Y25M018 immediate removal 1 → 0; Y25M033 Jul–Aug max days since spray before rain 4 → 8. 141 farms in total.
  • Changes from v4 (v7): Protectant spray counts corrected on 13 diseased farms (all lower; e.g. Y26M013-2 9 → 3, Y25M105 8 → 4, Y26M018 3 → 0). All sections were re-analysed on v7. Since protectant sprays are the only changed variable, the pre-specified model (Section 04) and the vulnerability model (Section 05), which do not use it, give the same estimates as on v4.
  • Occurrence label: Instead of the Y1 occurrence label, we defined occurrence (1) as Y2 total severity > threshold and everything else as 0. A threshold of 0 equals the original Y1.
  • Classification predictors: All variables from cultivar resistance to exposure, excluding first-symptom DOY, days transplant→first symptom and immediate removal (recorded only for diseased farms). The all-variable model has 44 candidates without exposure (including 8 weather variables and 2 IR-area variables); the no-weather model has 35 (exposure is the only weather information).
  • Procedure: Screening per threshold (2 variable sets × 2 selection methods × 4 models × 6 values of k, 5-fold × 5 repeats) → at the best threshold, 4 selection methods (mean rank, RF-SHAP, RFE, Boruta) × 6 models × k (5-fold × 6 repeats, selection inside each fold) → the final model is the setting with the fewest variables within 0.005 of the best AUC.

02Choosing the occurrence threshold

{{t_thr}}

For both variable sets, AUC was highest at threshold 0.03 (severity above 3%), with 0.015 close behind (PR-AUC was highest at 0.015). Following the requested criterion (AUC), we chose 0.03.

Changing the threshold changes the prevalence (43% → 28%) and what is being predicted, so AUC differences mean "which definition the predictors separate better". Because 0.03 is the edge of the search range, a larger threshold could do better still. PR-AUC has a baseline equal to the prevalence, so AUC is the fairer comparison across thresholds.

Exposure is used separately as the exposure (E) term of the vulnerability analysis in Section 05, so this model leaves exposure out and uses the remaining 44 variables (including 8 weather variables and 2 IR-area variables). The data are v7. Occurrence = severity > {{thr}} ({{n1}} farms). Final model: {{ca_cfg}}. AUC with variable selection inside each fold {{ca_auc}}; AUC with the selected variables fixed {{ca_auc_fixed}}. The same procedure on the same v7 data with exposure included (45 variables) gave AUC 0.821, so dropping exposure did not reduce predictive power. The information carried by exposure is taken over by the weather variables (rainfall, temperature, rainy days) and the rain–spray timing variables.

Effect sizes (pre-specified model, {{ca_nspec}} variables)

Odds ratios estimated after selecting variables on the same data are inflated, and their p-values and confidence intervals are not valid. We therefore fixed the variables in advance, independent of the results, using the vulnerability framework (exposure, sensitivity, adaptive capacity; Section 05), but left exposure out of this model: three sensitivity variables (disease last year, continuous cropping, nearby pepper field), two adaptive practices (days since spray before rain and weed mat, selected as in Section 05), farm size as an adjustment, and two weather representatives (July–August rainfall, June mean temperature). With only {{ca_epv}} events per variable, we used Firth-penalized logistic regression, which reduces coefficient inflation.

The confidence intervals of the sensitivity variables and farm size include 1. {{ca_stab}}

The two measures answer different questions. Stability-selection frequency asks "is the variable picked consistently when only a few are chosen among many correlated candidates?"; the Firth odds ratio asks "how large is the effect after adjusting for the pre-specified variables?". Days since spray before rain and July–August rainfall (frequency 0.58, p ≤ 0.012) are high on both. Protectant sprays are the most stable variable (frequency 0.87) but are not in the pre-specified model. The adaptive variables were chosen from the v4 comparison, where spray intensity added nothing once the days since spray before rain were included. With the corrected counts of 13 farms in v7 its signal became clearer (rank correlation −0.25 → −0.36), and in the Section 05 comparison the sets with protectant sprays did better. Changing pre-specified variables after seeing the results weakens the point of pre-specification, so the model was left unchanged for now.

In observed rates, 11% of farms with ≤120 mm of July–August rainfall exceeded 3% damage, against 40% above 200 mm. Farms with 0–1 protectant sprays: 47%; 5 or more: 11%. Farms with disease last year: 40% (20% without); weed-mat users: 21% (36% without).

05Vulnerability analysis: exposure × sensitivity × (1 − adaptive capacity)

We fitted a model of the occurrence probability (severity > 3%) with the same structure as the vulnerability formula. From it we computed the reduction in occurrence probability per practice, each variable's contribution, how practice effects change with exposure, and an adaptive-capacity index.

  • Exposure (E): the exposure value. Sensitivity (S): disease last year, continuous cropping and a pepper field within 50 m (conditions a farm cannot change in the short term), plus farm size (no. of plants) as an adjustment. Adaptive capacity (A): two practices, days since spray before rain and weed mat (see the comparison table below for the selection).
  • Practice score a (0 = worst, 1 = best): weed mat used = 1. Days since spray before rain (Jul–Aug max): 27 or more → 0, 2 or fewer → 1, linear in between.
  • Model: P(occurrence) = 1 − exp(−H), H = λ · Eα · S(E) · (1 − A). Adaptive capacity starts from the rule that practices act independently, A = 1 − Π(1 − wj·aj); a synergy or overlap term between the two practices was tested with a likelihood-ratio test. Uncertainty comes from 300 bootstrap resamples.

Selecting the adaptive-capacity components: comparison of candidate sets

In the no-weather RF model, spray-intensity variables such as July spray count, spray days and protectant sprays had high SHAP importance, while the weed mat and days since spray before rain ranked lower. We therefore compared 9 candidate sets in the same vulnerability model, including the top SHAP variables, spray-intensity representatives (spray days, or an index of 6 spray variables) and the previous stability-selection set, plus 2 sets with protectant sprays (S10, S11) added because this variable became the most important one in v7 (repeated stratified 5-fold × 10; weights from 200 bootstrap resamples).

{{v_t_adapt}}
  • Sets with protectant sprays did best. S11 (protectant sprays + weed mat) had the lowest AIC (143.6), both weights with 95% intervals excluding 0, and CV AUC 0.780. S10 (protectant sprays + days since spray before rain + weed mat) had the highest CV AUC (0.785), but the interval for days since spray before rain includes 0. S9 (days since spray before rain + weed mat), the set currently used, has CV AUC 0.759 and AIC 148.3. On the v4 data S9 was best (protectant weight 0.12 in S1 and S6). The vulnerability results below use S9; S10 and S11 were added after seeing the v7 results, so replacing the adaptive set needs a separate decision.
  • Overall spraying and protectant sprays act differently. Whenever days since spray before rain was in the model, the weights of overall spray intensity (spray days, July spray count, index) were 0 in every set, and using them instead of the timing variable lowered performance to 0.70–0.71 (S7, S8). Protectant sprays, by contrast, kept a weight of 1.0 (95% interval 0.29–1.0) even with the timing variable, whose weight fell from 0.84 to 0.57. The two are correlated (ρ −0.50) and share the effect: farms that sprayed protectants often were more likely to have fungicide on the plants just before rain.
  • Taking the SHAP ranking at face value (S2) did worst (AIC 155.8). The six spray variables repeat the same information (correlations 0.6–0.9), and RF splits importance among them, so several reach the top individually. RF SHAP also tends to rate binary variables (weed mat) low. SHAP is useful for finding which group of variables matters, but it is not the right criterion for choosing the independent components of a vulnerability formula.

The spray-intensity index is the first principal component of the 6 standardized spray variables and explains {{v_int_evr}}% of their variance. Loadings: {{v_int_load}}.

Tests of the model structure

  • Exposure raises the risk only when inoculum is present. The exposure exponent α is {{v_alpha}}, close to 0, and almost all of the exposure effect enters through the "disease last year × exposure" term (θx = {{v_thx}}, 95% interval 0.04–2.77). High-exposure farms with disease last year have an observed rate of 74% against a predicted 66%. Farms without disease last year have a current occurrence probability of 22–24% whether exposure is 0.1 or 0.9.
  • Multiplying by exposure linearly (α = 1) does not fit the data (CV AUC 0.64 vs 0.76). For the vulnerability calculation we therefore recommend the model's effective exposure, Eeff = Eα · exp(θx · disease last year · E), rescaled to 0–1, instead of the raw exposure value.
  • No synergy or overlap between the two practices was significant (p = 0.95). The independent-action rule 1 − Π(1 − w·a) is enough.
{{v_t_cv}}

The multiplicative model (0.759) matched or slightly beat the logistic model with exposure × practice interactions (0.743), and we chose it because it matches the vulnerability formula. It is below the full ML model (Section 04, 0.81), the price of keeping 7 variables and a fixed structure.

What "current practice" means. The practices the 141 farms actually reported in the survey, left as they are. Only exposure is set to the same value (0.1, 0.5 or 0.9) for every farm; practices (days since spray before rain, weed mat) and sensitivity (disease last year, continuous cropping, nearby pepper field, farm size) keep each farm's observed values. Vulnerability formula (occurrence = severity above 3%)

{{v_A_formula}}

Eeff = E{{v_alpha}} · exp({{v_thx}} · disease last year · E) / max

S = exp({{v_S_formula}}) / max

Vulnerability V = Eeff × S × (1 − A), occurrence probability ≈ 1 − exp(−λ·V′) (V′ = V before rescaling)

sizez = (log no. of plants − mean) / SD. A farm 1 SD larger has S multiplied by exp(−0.46) ≈ 0.63, an adjustment toward lower risk. Disease last year has a zero main-effect coefficient and enters only through its product with exposure (effective exposure).

{{v_t_auc}}

Mean adaptive capacity is {{v_A_mean}}, lower on diseased farms (0.55) than on disease-free farms (0.73). The originally proposed form, exposure × (1 − adaptive capacity), discriminates with only AUC 0.63, while the form with effective exposure and sensitivity reaches 0.84 (training data), so we recommend the latter as the vulnerability index.

Limitations: (1) With only 40 events, the weight intervals are wide. The interval for days since spray before rain (0.04–1.0) excludes 0, while the lower bound for the weed mat sits at the boundary (0–0.05 depending on the bootstrap). (2) This is observational survey data, so the reductions are associations predicted by the model, not causal effects. (3) Extreme scenarios such as "all worst" extrapolate beyond the data and are left out of the tables.

06Severity regression: model comparison (Y2)

We analysed the 60 diseased farms (severity > 0) of the v7 data. The response is Y2 total severity. First-symptom DOY and days transplant→first symptom were removed from the predictors. Earlier first symptoms naturally mean a longer epidemic and higher final severity, and a short interval from transplanting to first symptoms also means early first symptoms. Both correlate strongly with severity and were picked by the earlier models, but they describe the same thing as the response (disease progress), so they cannot explain which cropping or environmental factors raise damage. Immediate removal of infected fruit is a management response after onset and stays as a predictor (36 candidates).

{{t_models}}

ExtraTrees (tuned, 15 variables, 0.426) did best, followed by default ExtraTrees (0.417), Random Forest (0.374) and CatBoost/LightGBM (0.36). Linear and kernel models reached 0.33–0.36 with only the three core variables, about 0.07 below the tree models. Averaging the top 3 models (0.419) did not beat the single best model. Performance is higher overall than on the v4 data (best 0.392, linear 0.26–0.30), because correcting the protectant spray counts of 13 farms sharpened the signal of that variable.

R² (Y2 raw scale) converts log10 predictions back to proportions. RMSE is in severity proportion units.

We re-evaluated the representative model of each family under the same conditions (5-fold × 10 repeats, different seed).

Final severity model: log10(Y2) predicted by ExtraTrees (tuned, top 15 variables by mean rank). Repeated CV R² {{reg_r2}} (log10), 0.25 (raw scale), nested CV {{reg_nested}}. In every model, immediate removal of infected fruit was by far the most important variable, followed by anthracnose last year (more damage) and protectant sprays (less damage). Interpretive linear model: how many times does severity change?

{{t_coef}}

OLS regression of log10 severity on the three core variables chosen by both the linear and kernel models. Severity multiplier = 10^coefficient (other variables fixed). Farms that remove infected fruit immediately have about 0.24× the severity (95% interval 0.12–0.46), farms with anthracnose last year about 1.9× (1.08–3.18), and farms with 1 SD (3.5) more protectant sprays about 0.69× (0.49–0.96, p = 0.030). The protectant effect was not significant on the v4 data (0.83×, p = 0.26) but is significant on the corrected v7 data. The training R² of this model (0.52) is above its cross-validated R² (0.35–0.40), so read the direction and relative size rather than the exact multipliers. In the raw data, the median severity on farms that remove infected fruit immediately is 2.0%, about one-eighth of that on farms that do not (16.8%).

09Conclusions

  • Occurrence threshold: The predictors separate farms best when occurrence is defined as severity above 3% (AUC 0.82). Mild disease (≤ 3%) is hard to tell apart from no disease with these predictors.
  • Occurrence risk factors: June temperature and July–August rainfall raise the risk; a weed mat and spraying shortly before rain lower it. On the v7 data, farms with more protectant sprays had fewer occurrences (0–1 sprays 47%, 5 or more 11%), and this variable was selected most consistently. Weather variables without exposure (AUC {{ca_auc}}) and exposure alone instead of weather (AUC {{cn_auc}}) give similar accuracy, so an exposure-based model is the practical choice in the field.
  • Vulnerability: The highest-risk group is high-exposure farms that had anthracnose last year. On these farms, adaptive practices such as spraying shortly before rain (≤ 2 days) and using a weed mat lower the occurrence probability the most. On v7, protectant sprays are a strong variable for both occurrence and severity, and in the adaptive-set comparison protectant sprays + weed mat (S11) did better than the current set (S9) (AIC 143.6 vs 148.3). Adding protectant sprays to adaptive capacity is worth considering. The vulnerability index should use effective exposure, which accounts for disease last year, rather than raw exposure.
  • Severity: Leaving out onset timing, immediate removal of infected fruit after onset reduces damage the most (×0.24), farms with more protectant sprays have less damage (×0.69 per SD), and farms with anthracnose last year have more (×1.9). The remaining cropping and environmental variables explain about 36–43% of the variation in log severity, so much of the severity depends on onset timing and other factors not in the survey.
