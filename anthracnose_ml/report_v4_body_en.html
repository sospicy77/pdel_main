  • Occurrence threshold: We redefined occurrence from total severity using thresholds from 0 to 0.03 in steps of 0.005. Classification worked best when severity above 3% counts as occurrence (AUC 0.767 → 0.806). Farms with mild disease (severity ≤ 3%) look like disease-free farms in their predictors, so grouping them with the disease-free farms separates the two classes better.
  • All-variable model: The main predictors are anthracnose last year, weed mat, spray delay after rain, July–August rainfall, exposure and June temperature. In the pre-specified logistic model, June mean temperature (odds ×2.12 per SD, p = 0.008) raises the risk and a weed mat (×0.30, p = 0.020) lowers it; July–August rainfall (×1.73, p = 0.053) is borderline.
  • No-weather model: Dropping the 10 weather variables and keeping only exposure gives AUC {{cn_auc}}, only 0.014 below the all-variable model. Exposure carries most of the weather information. In this model the weed mat (×0.34, p = 0.024) is significant, and exposure (×1.47, p = 0.07) and farm size (no. of plants, ×0.63, p = 0.08) are borderline.
  • Vulnerability analysis (Section 05): Exposure raised the risk only on farms with anthracnose last year. At exposure 0.9, putting all four practices at their best lowers the occurrence probability from 38.9% to 15.6% (−23 pp), and from 56% to 24% (−32 pp) on farms with disease last year. Within adaptive capacity, keeping the days since the last spray before rain short (about 69%) and the weed mat (about 28%) carry most of the weight, and no synergy between practices was significant. The index Vulnerability = effective exposure × sensitivity × (1 − adaptive capacity) discriminates occurrence with AUC 0.84.
  • Severity is explained mostly by three variables: immediate removal of infected fruit (severity ×0.35), later first symptoms (×0.61 per 11 days) and the days from transplanting to first symptoms. Among 15 regression models on the v4 data, linear models (Huber, Ridge, ElasticNet; R² 0.56) did best, followed by the Gaussian process and ExtraTrees (0.53–0.54); boosting models reached 0.42–0.44. The nested CV R², free of selection bias, is 0.53 (Sections 06–08).

01Data and design

  • Changes from v3 (v4): Y25M005 (disease-free) removed; Y25M013 severity 0.05 → 0.25; Y25M018 immediate removal 1 → 0; Y25M033 Jul–Aug max days since spray before rain 4 → 8. 141 farms in total.
  • Occurrence label: Instead of the Y1 occurrence label, we defined occurrence (1) as Y2 total severity > threshold and everything else as 0. A threshold of 0 equals the original Y1.
  • Classification predictors: All variables from cultivar resistance to exposure, excluding first-symptom DOY, days transplant→first symptom and immediate removal (recorded only for diseased farms). The all-variable model has 45 candidates (including 8 weather variables and 2 IR-area variables); the no-weather model has 35 (exposure is the only weather information).
  • Procedure: Screening per threshold (2 variable sets × 2 selection methods × 4 models × 6 values of k, 5-fold × 5 repeats) → at the best threshold, 4 selection methods (mean rank, RF-SHAP, RFE, Boruta) × 6 models × k (5-fold × 6 repeats, selection inside each fold) → the final model is the setting with the fewest variables within 0.005 of the best AUC.

02Choosing the occurrence threshold

{{t_thr}}

For both variable sets, AUC was highest at threshold 0.03 (severity above 3%), with 0.015 close behind (PR-AUC and balanced accuracy were highest at 0.015). Following the requested criterion (AUC), we chose 0.03.

Changing the threshold changes the prevalence (43% → 28%) and what is being predicted, so AUC differences mean "which definition the predictors separate better". Because 0.03 is the edge of the search range, a larger threshold could do better still. PR-AUC has a baseline equal to the prevalence, so AUC is the fairer comparison across thresholds.

03Occurrence model: all variables

Occurrence = severity > {{thr}} ({{n1}} farms). Final model: {{ca_cfg}}. AUC with variable selection inside each fold {{ca_auc}}; AUC with the selected variables fixed {{ca_auc_fixed}}.

Effect sizes (pre-specified model, {{ca_nspec}} variables)

Odds ratios estimated after selecting variables on the same data are inflated, and their p-values and confidence intervals are not valid. We therefore fixed the variables in advance, independent of the results, using the vulnerability framework (exposure, sensitivity, adaptive capacity; Section 05): exposure, three sensitivity variables (disease last year, continuous cropping, nearby pepper field), four adaptive practices (days since spray before rain, spray delay after rain, weed mat, protectant sprays) and farm size as an adjustment, plus two weather representatives (July–August rainfall, June mean temperature). With only {{ca_epv}} events per variable, we used Firth-penalized logistic regression, which reduces coefficient inflation.

{{ca_or}}

June mean temperature (×2.12 per SD, p = 0.008) and the weed mat (×0.30, p = 0.020) were significant, and July–August rainfall (×1.73, p = 0.053) was borderline. The other variables point in the expected direction, but their confidence intervals include 1. All VIFs are below 2.

Strength of evidence for each variable. Stability selection frequencies over all {{ca_ncand}} candidate variables of the final ML model (500 half-size subsamples × L1 regularization path). The regularization range is limited so that on average at most 5 variables are selected; with a frequency cut-off of 0.7, the expected number of falsely selected variables is at most {{ca_ev}}. Frequency ≥ 0.7 is labelled "strong", 0.4–0.7 "moderate" and below 0.4 "weak".

{{ca_stab}}

The two measures answer different questions. Stability selection frequency asks "is the variable picked consistently when only a few of many correlated candidates are chosen?"; the Firth odds ratio asks "how large is its effect after adjusting for the pre-specified variables?". For example, the weed mat has a low frequency (0.30) but a significant adjusted effect (p = 0.02), while July–August rainfall has a high frequency (0.61) and a borderline adjusted effect. Evidence is strongest for variables that score high on both.

In raw rates, 11% of farms with ≤ 120 mm of July–August rain exceeded 3% severity, against 40% of farms with > 200 mm. The rate was 40% on farms with disease last year (20% without) and 21% on farms using a weed mat (36% without).

05Vulnerability analysis: exposure × sensitivity × (1 − adaptive capacity)

We fitted a model of the occurrence probability (severity > 3%) with the same structure as the vulnerability formula. From it we computed the reduction in occurrence probability per practice, each variable's contribution, how practice effects change with exposure, and an adaptive-capacity index.

  • Exposure (E): the exposure value. Sensitivity (S): disease last year, continuous cropping and a pepper field within 50 m (conditions a farm cannot change in the short term), plus farm size (no. of plants) as an adjustment. Adaptive capacity (A): four practices that bootstrap stability selection (L1 logistic, 300 resamples) picked consistently: days since spray before rain (89%), weed mat (81%), protectant sprays (51%) and spray delay after rain (43%).
  • Practice score a (0 = worst, 1 = best): weed mat used = 1. Protectant sprays: 0 → 0, 10 or more → 1. Days since spray before rain (Jul–Aug max): 27 or more → 0, 2 or fewer → 1. Spray delay after rain (max): 45 or more → 0, 1 or fewer → 1. Linear in between.
  • Model: P(occurrence) = 1 − exp(−H), H = λ · Eα · S(E) · (1 − A). Adaptive capacity starts from the rule that practices act independently, A = 1 − Π(1 − wj·aj); pairwise synergy or overlap terms were added only if a likelihood-ratio test supported them. Uncertainty comes from 300 bootstrap resamples.

Tests of the model structure

  • Exposure raises the risk only when inoculum is present. The exposure exponent α is {{v_alpha}}, close to 0, and almost all of the exposure effect enters through the "disease last year × exposure" term (θx = {{v_thx}}). Without this term, the model predicted 53% for high-exposure farms with disease last year (observed 74%); with it, 66% (likelihood-ratio test p ≈ 0.03). Farms without disease last year have a current occurrence probability of 22–25% whether exposure is 0.1 or 0.9.
  • Multiplying by exposure linearly (α = 1) does not fit the data (CV AUC 0.64 vs 0.74). For the vulnerability calculation we therefore recommend the model's effective exposure, Eeff = Eα · exp(θx · disease last year · E), rescaled to 0–1, instead of the raw exposure value.
  • No synergy or overlap between practices was significant (all 6 pairs p > 0.27). The independent-action rule 1 − Π(1 − w·a) is enough. Under this rule a second practice adds less once the risk is already low, which shows up as a negative joint contribution in the Shapley table below (weed mat × days since spray before rain, −0.15).
{{v_t_cv}}

The multiplicative model and the logistic model with exposure × practice interactions have the same CV AUC (0.74), so the data cannot tell the two structures apart; we chose the multiplicative one because it matches the vulnerability formula. Both are below the full ML model (Section 04, 0.79), the price of keeping 9 variables and a fixed structure.

How practice effects change with exposure

In the multiplicative structure, the relative reduction does not depend on exposure, and the absolute reduction (pp) grows with the baseline risk. In the logistic model that estimates exposure × practice interactions directly, the protectant-spray effect grew from 4 pp to 14 pp with exposure. Two models with the same performance split the effect between practices differently, so the split between practices is uncertain while the total adaptive effect is robust.

Adaptive-capacity index and vulnerability

{{v_t_w}}

Vulnerability formula (occurrence = severity above 3%)

A = 1 − (1 − 0.82·adays since spray before rain)(1 − 0.51·aweed mat)(1 − 0.12·aprotectant)(1 − 0·adelay after rain)

Eeff = E{{v_alpha}} · exp({{v_thx}} · disease last year · E) / max, S = exp(0.15·continuous cropping + 0.41·nearby pepper field − 0.46·sizez) / max

Vulnerability V = Eeff × S × (1 − A), occurrence probability ≈ 1 − exp(−λ·V′) (V′ = V before rescaling)

{{v_t_auc}}

Mean adaptive capacity is {{v_A_mean}}, lower on diseased farms (0.56) than on disease-free farms (0.73). The originally proposed form, exposure × (1 − adaptive capacity), discriminates with only AUC 0.63, while the form with effective exposure and sensitivity reaches 0.84 (training data), so we recommend the latter as the vulnerability index.

Limitations: (1) With only 40 events, the 95% intervals of the practice weights are wide. The ranking (days since spray before rain > weed mat > protectant sprays) is indicative; what is robust is the effect of the practices together. (2) This is observational survey data, so the reductions are associations predicted by the model, not causal effects. For example, if high-risk farms sprayed protectants more, the protectant effect is underestimated. (3) Extreme scenarios such as "all worst" extrapolate beyond the data and are left out of the tables.

06Severity regression: model comparison (Y2)

We reanalysed the 60 diseased farms (severity > 0) of the v4 data. The response is Y2 total severity, and the predictors include first-symptom DOY, days transplant→first symptom and immediate removal of infected fruit. Compared with v3, Y25M013 severity (0.05 → 0.25) and Y25M018 immediate removal (1 → 0) changed.

{{t_models}}

Linear models did best (Huber 0.559, Ridge 0.558, ElasticNet 0.557), followed by the Gaussian process (0.539), Kernel Ridge (0.534) and ExtraTrees (0.527). Boosting models (XGBoost, CatBoost, LightGBM, GBM) reached only 0.42–0.44. Averaging the top 3 models (0.563) did not beat the single models. Every model family improved on v3 (best 0.520).

R² (Y2 raw scale) converts log10 predictions back to proportions. The raw-scale R² is lower than the log-scale R² because a few heavily damaged farms dominate it. RMSE is in severity proportion units (0.11 ≈ 11 pp).

07Final severity model and interpretation

We re-evaluated the best model of each family under the same conditions (5-fold × 10 repeats, different seed). All chose the same three variables and performed similarly, so the conclusions do not depend on one model.

{{t_final}}

Final severity model: log10(Y2) ~ immediate removal of infected fruit + first-symptom DOY + days transplant→first symptom (Huber regression). Repeated CV R² 0.58 (log10), 0.46 (raw scale). Huber regression is a robust linear regression that down-weights farms with large errors. The Gaussian process (0.58) and ExtraTrees (0.55) with the same three variables do almost as well, so non-linear effects and interactions are small.

Huber regression coefficients: how many times does severity change?

{{t_coef}}

Severity multiplier = 10^coefficient, holding the other two variables fixed. Farms that remove infected fruit immediately have about 0.35× the severity (v3: 0.44×), and first symptoms 11 days later mean about 0.61×. First-symptom DOY and days transplant→first symptom are strongly correlated (ρ 0.86), so read their two coefficients together as the effect of "timing of first symptoms".

09Conclusions

  • Occurrence threshold: The predictors separate farms best when occurrence is defined as severity above 3% (AUC 0.81). Mild disease (≤ 3%) is hard to tell apart from no disease with these predictors.
  • Occurrence risk factors: June temperature, July–August rainfall and disease last year raise the risk; a weed mat and spraying promptly around rain lower it. Using exposure alone instead of the weather variables gives nearly the same accuracy (AUC 0.79), so an exposure-based model is the practical choice in the field.
  • Vulnerability: The highest-risk group is high-exposure farms that had anthracnose last year. On these farms, adaptive practices such as spraying shortly before rain (≤ 2 days) and using a weed mat lower the occurrence probability the most. The vulnerability index should use effective exposure, which accounts for disease last year, rather than raw exposure.
  • Severity: Immediate removal of infected fruit and the timing of first symptoms determine the severity after onset; three variables explain about 53–58% of the variation in log severity (v3: 46–52%). The v4 corrections removed the prediction errors of the corrected farms.
